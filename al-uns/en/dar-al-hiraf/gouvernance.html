DAR AL-HIRAF

At-Tadbir

التدبير

The governance of the Riad — who trains, who decides, who answers.

At-tadbīr (التدبير) is not a term chosen at random. It is not the modern calque of "governance" — al-ḥawkama, imported from the vocabulary of the corporation — but the classical word that Islamic political thought reserved for the government of the household: the tadbīr al-manzil that Ibn Sīnā set, at the scale of the home, as the counterpart to the siyāsa that governs the city. The Riad thinks of itself as a house, not as an institution: this was the word it needed.

This page describes who holds which responsibility at Dar al-Hiraf — not an abstract organisational chart, but a chain of real people, each recognisable by their dress (see Al-Kiswa) as much as by their function.

THE CHAIN

From apprentice to the Ash-Shaykh

1

The trainee — the three tones of leather

Each resident enters as an apprentice in a workshop and moves through the three tones of leather of the Hizam al-Itqan, the grade belt — beige, cognac, brown. Before the first leather he wears no belt; the gold thread lies outside the scale and belongs to the Ash-Shaykh alone. Nothing is gained by seniority: progression follows competence actually shown.

2

Al-Naib — the deputy

One of the most advanced trainees in each guild carries out the function of nāʾib (النائب, "deputy"): he assists the others when the maalem is occupied or absent, without ever substituting for his authority on matters of training or discipline. A responsibility, not a separate rank.

3

The maalem — the master of the craft

One maalem per discipline, responsible for his own workshop and for the trainees entrusted to it. Selection weighs technical competence, uprightness and a verified reputation — the house favours young masters, for whom the charge is a source of autonomy and professional pride, with no rigid age ceiling: competence and uprightness take precedence over seniority alone.

See Masters & staff wanted.

4

Al-Muqaddam — the two advisors

Two muqaddam (المقدّمون) permanently assist the Ash-Shaykh — one for the inner life of the house, the other for outside relations. Resident at his side, in a space of their own reserved for leadership, they belong to no guild in particular: their dress, entirely black, signals this unambiguously.

5

Ash-Shaykh — the head of the house

At the summit, the Ash-Shaykh wears the colour of no single craft: he does not belong to one guild, he represents them all. His authority has no need to be signalled by a scale of grades — it is already whole, and is read in the belt embroidered with gold thread that he wears permanently, the only one to do so outside investiture ceremonies.

THE CHARGES

Neither craft nor rank

Six offices of residents, and they are whole days’ work: none of them is added to a day in a workshop. They are held for two years. Two others carry a colour without belonging to them: the muezzin, who keeps his workshop and takes the cord alone, and the standard-bearer, which falls to a man for a single year. Each is taken up with a colour — the tone of the charge on the jacket, the coat and the cord, the cap keeping the workshop and the belt the grade — and with an object, which is not carried about but passed from hand to hand on the day it is taken and on the day it moves on. The dress is set out in Al-Kiswa.

Al-Bawwab

The one who holds the door. Ochre of the ramparts. The key of the gate. See Threshold & rooms.

Al-Khazin

The one who holds the volumes. Ink-brown. The key of the khizana. See Al-Khizana.

Al-Jannan

The one who holds the gardens. Deep green. See Garden & kitchen garden.

الوكيل

Al-Wakil

The one who holds the household economy — provisions, store, table, accounts. Oil-brown. The key of the store. The dress is set out in Al-Kiswa.

الفرّاش

Al-Farash

The one who holds the rooms, the linen and the beds — and the wardrobe of the whole house. Flax grey. The keys of the rooms. The dress is set out in Al-Kiswa.

قيّم الحمّام

Al-Qayyim al-hammam

The one who holds the hammam. Light ash. The key of the hammam. See The hammam.

THE CRITERION

How authority states itself

The criterion of authority

The house holds together two things usually thought to be opposed: a real hierarchy, and an equality of substance. A function, a skill, means of one’s own do not make the man who holds them more important — they oblige him further. Everything here is ordered to the common good, and nothing to the pride of the one who exercises it. Higher and lower, by charge, by talent or by fortune, are bound together by futuwwa, by brotherhood and by furūsiyya.

Authority does not state itself by exempting itself from humble tasks. It states itself by accepting them without fear.

The nāʾib, the maalem, the muqaddam and the shaykh discharge, when the house requires it, the same offices as the ṣāniʿ. A man whose standing is secure only on condition that he never perform a subordinate task does not yet have authority: he wears its badge, nothing more.

The sani

The ṣāniʿ is the first degree of the chain, not a lesser being. He comes in of age, receives a craft and a discipline, and holds his place at the table as in the workshop.

This holds only if both men know it. If the younger took the master’s service as a victory over him, the gesture would already be lost.

Nearness of age

The maalem and the faqīh are chosen older than the ṣāniʿ, without the gap amounting to a barrier of generations. The muqaddam and the shaykh are elected from among their peers.

Authority is not earned by the mere passing of time, but by the maturity a man has reached through effort and discipline. He who cannot command himself cannot command others.

The passions are not put out. They are bridled — as the ṣāniʿ learns to break a colt still untamed: not to crush its strength, but to give it a direction.

Furusiyya

Furūsiyya gives this rule a body. The ṣāniʿ are made to know, physically, what it is to hold an animal not yet tamed, so that they understand in the gesture itself what commanding oneself means.

The bit is no accident of the harness: it is the figure of the check a man sets on whatever in him would carry him away. The passions are not evil in themselves. They become so by the use one makes of them.

The colt that rears and tries to unseat its rider is like the man entering Dar al-Hiraf. The community supplies him with the bit, the bridle, the spurs and the saddle. But it is he who must hold on.

The same exercise

This principle is also the model for the presence of the guests of Al-Munya within the community. Their stay does not rest on what they own. It rests on this exercise: commanding oneself, setting aside rank, name, fortune, office and the prestige the world attaches to such things — in order to be, here, only oneself.

That is not lowering oneself. It is stepping down from a pedestal in order to rise more truly, and higher.

Two registers, not one

The house distinguishes what touches persons from what touches the work.

At-Tahkim — the arbitration of tensions of honour between peers and, if need be, the trial under rule. It closes a dispute between men. It does not judge a panel of zellige, nor a grade.

The work and the grade — Athar, the ijāza, the eye of the maalem: this is where it is said whether a gesture holds and whether a man rises. The trade council, provided for by the rule, belongs to this second register. It is not a commercial label.

WHAT THE HIERARCHY DOES NOT DO

A chain of responsibility, not of distance

The spaces of educators and masters remain physically distinct from those of the residents, and no meeting between a responsible adult and a young resident is held one-on-one in an isolated place: exchanges of training, advice or correction take place in common, visible spaces — workshop, study room, courtyard. The personal relationship between master and student, essential to the transmission of the craft, is lived fully within this shared setting, never in isolation.

Lessons in doctrine (Qur'an, fiqh, ʿaqīda) remain separate: they are given by a resident faqīh, who shares the rhythm of the house without belonging to the chain of the crafts. His charge is the hours of prayer and the teaching — he does not decide admissions, does not arbitrate disputes and holds no power of sanction. His lessons are held, like everything else, in common and visible spaces.

This page gives the broad outline. The full detail — selection of masters, procedure in case of misconduct, what falls under each level — is set out in the community life rule given to each resident upon admission.

WHAT IS NEEDED

What the house lives on

No one here is paid for what he does. A resident is not an employee: he receives lodging, the table and everything he needs, with nothing withheld from him and nothing counted against him. Clothing, tools, care and books come from the house.

The necessary being covered, each man has a personal allowance for what is not — and more of it if he asks. Alongside that, a savings account accumulates in his name throughout his years of residence. It is not deferred wages: it is provision, so that a man leaves here with something in front of him rather than empty-handed.

Who holds what

Bookkeeping and tax are entrusted to an outside firm, which has no role in the house, does not live in it and does not teach in it: it keeps the books, nothing else, and answers only to the Ash-Shaykh and the wakīl.

Within, the wakīl holds the stores, the table and the ordinary accounts; the Ash-Shaykh answers for the rest. The savings are deposited outside and in each man’s own name: they are in nobody’s hands here, and no one has to ask permission for them.

What it earns, what it receives

The house earns its living first. By the work of its workshops, whose production is sold; and by the share paid by the guests of Al-Munya and the members of its circle — the very share that pays for the free places.

It receives next: study bursaries, the support of its patrons — gifts and bequests —, institutional funds, and the contribution of those families able to give one.

No one of these sources is enough on its own, and that is deliberate: a house that hangs on a single source hangs on whoever holds it.

Those who have nothing

A young man without means is received free of charge, and nothing in the house sets him apart: not the room, not the place at table, not the clothing, which is handed to him complete on arrival as it is to everyone.

What makes this possible is not alms taken out of the rest. It is Al-Munya: the guests who stay there pay, and pay dearly, and that price pays for the free places. The house has not set charity beside its economy — it has made its economy the instrument of its charity.

Those who come from outside

Salaried masters, professionals and tradesmen called in for what the house cannot do itself are paid for their work and go home when it is done. The rule holds in one sentence: no one who lives here is paid, no one who is paid lives here.

THE THRESHOLD

How one approaches the house

Governance assumes men who have crossed the threshold in order: read, write, the exchange, the training or the stay, then the residency. It is not an enrolment.
