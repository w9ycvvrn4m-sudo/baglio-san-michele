DAR AL-HIRAF

Futuwwa

الفتوة

The ideal that gives form and direction to Riad Al-Uns.

THE NAME AND ITS MEANING

The fata and his quality

Fata (فتى) means “young man.” Futuwwa is the quality of the fata — not youth in the sense of age, but an inner disposition: the capacity to be generous to the point of self-sacrifice, loyal unto death, courageous without vanity, master of oneself before being master of others.

In the classical tradition, futuwwa is not a feeling. It is a path — with its rules, degrees, signs, an ethics of body and speech, and an initiation.

ROOTS

A history, not a legend

Futuwwa arose in the urban Abbasid milieu and developed between the 10th and 13th centuries. It organised itself into brotherhoods of young men — often craftsmen, sometimes soldiers or urban guards — bound by a pact of fraternity and a rigorous code of conduct.

The caliph al-Nasir (late 12th – early 13th century) sought to institutionalise it across the Islamic world. Manuals of futuwwa appeared, describing the required virtues, rules of common life, the master–disciple relationship, outward signs — belt, garment, the way of tying the shadd — and degrees of commitment.

Many of these brotherhoods were linked to craft guilds (asnaf). The craft was never only a technique: it was the vehicle for transmitting an ethic. Learning to work wood, metal or cloth meant learning to “hold” — a gesture, a given word, a responsibility.

A STABLE CORE

A stable core

Beyond historical variations, a few virtues form the stable core of futuwwa — still readable today, still formable in the body and in daily work.

Sakha

Generosity — not the gift that expects return, but the readiness to give of oneself, of time, of skill, without calculation.

Wafa

Fidelity — to the given word, to the brother, to the craft, to the pact that binds the community.

Shajaa

Courage — without vanity: the strength to hold when it costs, not the display of force.

Sidq

Truthfulness — speech that matches the deed; the refusal of the double game.

Mastery of the nafs

Self-mastery before mastery of others — the body and desire trained, not denied.

Service

To serve the brother, the craft, the house — not as humiliation, but as the form of honour.

INITIATION

Initiation and signs

Entry into futuwwa was not a mere declaration. It involved a relationship of transmission — master and disciple — outward signs (belt, garment, the knot of the shadd), and degrees of commitment that structured the path over years.

At Dar al-Hiraf these signs find a contemporary form in the clothing system of the house — see Al-Kiswa — without becoming costume or folklore.

CRAFT AND PATH

Futuwwa and Dar al-Hiraf

Dar al-Hiraf does not reconstruct a medieval order. It takes from futuwwa what remains formative: the craft as school of character, shared life as training in loyalty, the body as the place where measure is learned before it is spoken.

The residency, the workshops, the daily rhythm of prayer and work are the concrete framework of that path.

BODY

Futuwwa and physical disciplines

Medieval manuals of futuwwatname codified wrestling, archery and horsemanship as formative disciplines on the same footing as craft and prayer — the trained body is never separate from the character it shapes. These three disciplines were not mere physical training: they taught the fata measure in effort, control of breath before control of the gesture, and respect for the opponent or the mount as an extension of the respect due to others.

Dar al-Hiraf follows the same logic: force without measure is not futuwwa — it is its negation. A rider who brutalises his mount, an archer who shoots without inner discipline, a wrestler who seeks to humiliate rather than to measure his strength — all betray through the body what speech or craft might betray otherwise.

Equitation, archery and Musara (wrestling) are fully developed in Furusiyya, the Arab-Islamic chivalric discipline already present on the site.

TODAY

A possibility of form

Futuwwa is not a nostalgia exercise. It is a possibility of form for young men who refuse both the formless and the merely technical — who seek a craft, a brotherhood, and a measure that holds in daily life.

That is what Riad Al-Uns and Dar al-Hiraf propose to embody, without folklore and without compromise on the demand.
