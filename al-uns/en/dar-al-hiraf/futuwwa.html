DAR AL-HIRAF

Futuwwa

الفتوة

The ideal that gives form and direction to Riad Al-Uns.

THE NAME AND ITS MEANING

The fata and his quality

Fata (فتى) means “young man.” Futuwwa is the quality of the fata — not youth in the sense of age, but an inner disposition: the capacity to be generous to the point of self-sacrifice, loyal unto death, courageous without vanity, master of oneself before being master of others.

In the classical tradition, futuwwa is not a feeling. It is a path — with its rules, degrees, signs, an ethics of body and speech, and an initiation.

ROOTS

A history, not a legend

Futuwwa arose in the urban Abbasid milieu and developed between the 10th and 13th centuries. It organised itself into brotherhoods of young men — often craftsmen, sometimes soldiers or urban guards — bound by a pact of fraternity and a rigorous code of conduct.

The caliph al-Nasir (late 12th – early 13th century) sought to institutionalise it across the Islamic world. Manuals of futuwwa appeared, describing the required virtues, rules of common life, the master–disciple relationship, outward signs — belt, garment, the way of tying the shadd (شد) — and degrees of commitment.

Many of these brotherhoods were linked to craft guilds (asnaf). The craft was never only a technique: it was the vehicle for transmitting an ethic. Learning to work wood, metal or cloth meant learning to “hold” — a gesture, a given word, a responsibility.

Futuwwa is in intimate dialogue with Sufism, without merging into it. It is more earthly, more oriented towards action and service in the city. The ideal fatā is one who protects, who gives, who does not betray, who knows how to contain his own strength.

THE VIRTUES

A stable core

The classical texts insist on six requirements. Some — loyalty, courage, discipline — are developed in greater depth, through lived experience and companionship, in Brotherhood & uprightness. Here is the full doctrinal core:

سخاء

Sakha

Sakhāʾ — radical generosity: giving before being asked, giving what costs. In pre-Islamic Arab tradition, Ḥātim al-Ṭāʾī became its absolute embodiment — so much so that the expression akram min Ḥātim (أكرم من حاتم, “more generous than Ḥātim”) remains, to this day, the ultimate measure of munificence in the Arabic language.

وفاء

Wafa

Wafāʾ — fidelity to the given word, to the master, to the brothers, to the pact — until it costs, especially then. The pre-Islamic poet al-Samawʾal ibn ʿĀdiyā, who preferred to watch his own son die rather than betray a trust placed in his keeping, bequeathed to the Arabic language the expression awfā min al-Samawʾal (أوفى من السموأل, “more faithful than al-Samawʾal”). In one of his most quoted verses:

إذ المَرْءُ لَمْ يَدْنَسْ مِنَ اللُّؤْمِ عِرْضُهُ
فَكُلُّ رِدَاءٍ يَرْتَدِيهِ جَمِيلُ

“When a man's honor is unstained by any baseness, every garment he wears becomes him.”

شجاعة

Shajaa

Shajāʿa — courage, not only in the face of danger but in self-mastery — the capacity to contain one's own strength rather than deploy it without measure. Al-Mutanabbī put it thus, in a verse that became proverbial:

عَلى قَدْرِ أَهْلِ العَزْمِ تَأْتِي العَزَائِمُ

“Resolutions rise to the measure of those who hold them.”

صدق

Sidq

Ṣidq — truthfulness: word and deed coincide, without discrepancy, without exception of circumstance. An authentic hadith (reported by Bukhârî and Muslim) states it plainly:

عَلَيْكُمْ بِالصِّدْقِ فَإِنَّ الصِّدْقَ يَهْدِي إِلَى البِرِّ

“Hold to truthfulness, for truthfulness leads to righteousness.”

النفس

Mastery of the nafs

Strength without mastery is nothing but violence. The nafs (النفس) — the lower soul, the seat of impulses and pride — is what the fatā learns to govern before claiming to govern anything else: his workshop, his household, one day perhaps a family.

الخدمة

Service

The fatā exists for others before existing for himself. This is not self-renunciation, but an order of priorities: competence, strength, and generosity have meaning only when placed in service of something greater than oneself.

These virtues are not abstract “values.” They are exercised daily: at table, in work, in prayer, in silence, in the way one speaks and walks.

WHAT CAN BE SEEN

Initiation and signs

Traditional futuwwa involved a formal passage. The young man was accepted, received a belt tied in a precise manner, sometimes a particular garment. These signs were not costumes — they were the visible memory of a commitment made.

This is where one understands why, at Riad Al-Uns, clothing (Al-Kiswa) is not incidental. The garment, the belt of rank, the way of covering the head, the distinction between work attire and solemn attire belong to the same logic: making visible and constant what one has decided to be.

THE LINK WITH THE CRAFT

Futuwwa and Dar al-Hiraf

The link with Dar al-Hiraf is direct. In tradition, the master of a craft did not transmit only technical gestures — he transmitted an adab (أدب), a way of being in the craft and in life. The disciple learned to “hold” his craft as one holds a responsibility: with precision, with patience, with honour.

This is the formative heart of the Riad: not to produce beautiful objects, but to form men capable of carrying a craft and, one day, a family. Men who know how to endure.

THE TRAINED BODY

Futuwwa and physical disciplines

Medieval manuals of futuwwatname codified wrestling, archery and horsemanship as formative disciplines on the same footing as craft and prayer — the trained body is never separate from the character it shapes. These three disciplines were not mere physical training: they taught the fata measure in effort, control of breath before control of the gesture, and respect for the opponent or the mount as an extension of the respect due to others.

Dar al-Hiraf follows the same logic: force without measure is not futuwwa — it is its negation. A rider who brutalises his mount, an archer who shoots without inner discipline, a wrestler who seeks to humiliate rather than to measure his strength — all betray through the body what speech or craft might betray otherwise.

Equitation, archery and Musara (wrestling) are fully developed in Furusiyya, the Arab-Islamic chivalric discipline already present on the site.

WHY TODAY

A possibility of form

Historical futuwwa has almost disappeared as an institution. It remains, however, as a possibility of form.

Riad Al-Uns is not an antiquarian reconstruction. It is a contemporary attempt to recover the formative power of this ideal within a community of single young adult men: a prayer that structures the day, manual work that disciplines character, a common life under a rule of beauty and measure, a preparation for adult responsibility.

Futuwwa, in this sense, is not a subject of study. It is the horizon toward which the whole house is oriented.

The fatā is not the one who feels young.

He is the one who has decided to become worthy of being called a man.
