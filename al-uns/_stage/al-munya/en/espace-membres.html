An-Nudama

الندماء

A hundred less one. The hundredth place belongs to no one.

What the name means

The nadīm was the man admitted to the sovereign's private company in the evening. Neither official nor servant: his charge was to be there. The old manuals set down what was expected of him — bearing, an even face, learning, measure, patience, humility, few words, and above all the capacity to keep what he had heard.

المجالس بالأمانة

Assemblies are held in trust.

The member is a nadīm; the assembly, Majlis an-Nudamāʾ. The name does not say what the member already is. It says what he is asked to become.

What cannot be bought

Here no one is obliged to answer. Not because the telephone is forbidden — it is not — but because within these walls there is no one who wants anything from you. It is a condition money does not procure: you can buy an island and remain reachable there, for it is not places that make a man reachable, it is the people he brings with him.

On the other side of the wall lives a man of your age who owns nothing that can be sold and needs nothing you could give. He does not recognise you, does not know your trade, will never ask you for anything, and tomorrow will not seek you out.

When you sit at the same table, at the same hour, hands in the same plaster, what brings you together is neither a merit nor a quality: it is that at that moment neither of you can make use of the other.

It has no price, for want of a seller.

The rule of the house

The threshold

The house next door is not a place of entertainment and its inhabitants are not staff. They are given no tip, are not photographed, are not asked for demonstrations. What is granted — an hour at a bench, a meal at the common table, an evening of song — is granted case by case, appears on no list, is included in no price, and may be refused without reason. The member who breaches this rule sees his stay end that same day.

The hours

Five times a day the house stops. No one is obliged to pray; everyone is obliged to be silent. From ʿishāʾ to fajr, the house does not speak.

Dress

In the common spaces the member wears the dress of the house: undyed linen or hemp, no embroidery, nothing at the waist and nothing on the head. It is the same for everyone, provided and kept by the house, and never leaves it. It is worn without watch or visible jewellery. No sign of the dress of the house next door may be worn: those signs are earned through training and by a master's attestation.

The casket

The member is offered the chance to leave his telephone. What is left is locked away and entrusted to a man of the house next door: not a cloakroom but a deposit held in trust. It is returned to whoever asks, without comment, as many times as he asks. No one is obliged, and no one is looked at askance.

The table

One table, one menu, the season. No business is done in the house: no activity is presented, no card is handed over, nothing is signed, no meeting is held, and no one is brought in order to be introduced to another. What two men who became companions here decide outside is no concern of the house: it does not know and does not ask. The house gives you the men; it does not give you the meeting.

Length of stay

Three nights at least, fifteen at most. Beyond that, the house asks a different question.

Discretion

No name is published: neither of members, nor of neighbours, nor of anyone who has been received. No photograph is taken in the house. What is seen here is not reported. Indiscretion is grounds for removal, without refund.

The old rule sets its own limits, and the house takes them up: discretion covers neither harm done to a person, nor abuse, nor the taking of another's property. In those three cases, silence is itself a grave breach.

The closure clause

The house may be closed for up to thirty nights a year without a reason being given.

The member is told as soon as possible. Nights displaced are returned to him twofold, valid for eighteen months.

It is not customary to ask why, and no answer will be given. Speculating about it outside is a breach.

Presenting a candidate

One does not put oneself forward; one is presented. Two members present together, and stake their name.

  1. The two sponsors write to the house. One page is enough: who this man is, how long they have known him, why they answer for him.
  2. The candidate comes once. He shares a meal at the common table of the house next door, like any guest: without title, without being introduced, with no one knowing why he is there.
  3. The following day the house asks the table. Not a vote — an opinion. A single reservation is enough.
  4. The decision is given to the sponsors. It is never explained, neither to them nor to the candidate.

Admission requires the favourable opinion of the table.

The place

A place is never sold. Not to another member, not to a third party, not for value in any form whatsoever. Whoever attempts it loses the place and what he has paid.

An ordinary place is not transmitted: on death or resignation it returns to the house.

The forty founders are the exception. Each may designate his successor — a son, a younger brother, a man who is not of his family — in writing deposited with the register, or by testamentary disposition.

  • • It is a right of presentation, not of admission: the designated man sits the same interview, the same meal and the same opinion of the table, and meets the same conditions, age included.
  • • The successor does not pay the admission fee — that is what the founder bought. He pays the annual subscription.
  • • No money passes between the parties. If it appears the designation was paid for, it lapses.

There is no limit to the number of transmissions. A founder's place may descend through three generations, provided that each time a man sits before the house and the house says yes.

The register

The names of members exist in no digital form.

The roll, the acts of admission, the signatures, the designations of successors and the removals are kept by hand in a single register, held in the treasury of the house next door. No website, no list, no database, no scan.

Day-to-day administration works by number. What the law requires to be kept is kept elsewhere, and contains nothing beyond what the law requires.

What the subscription supports

A fixed share of the house's result is assigned by statute to the training bursaries of the house next door.

Two stays of seven nights keep an apprentice for a whole year.

The member knows which craft he supports, and may choose it. He will not know the apprentice's name, and that will not change: a young man who knows himself kept by a powerful man ceases to be held by his house and becomes someone's protégé.

Once a year the member receives a handwritten line saying what was done — a panel laid, a door finished, a master kept. He may go and see it, and will find it in the same place in twenty years.

For any request — a stay, a presentation, a designation.
