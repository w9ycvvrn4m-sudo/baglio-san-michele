MEDITERRANEAN GENEALOGY AND BUILDING KNOWLEDGE

Historical Roots of the Baglio

A centuries-old tradition of Mediterranean rural settlements that, faced with scarcity of resources and the harshness of the climate, knew how to build forms of life that were self-sufficient, balanced and socially cohesive.

Pietro Laureano · UNESCO Consultant

«Every day the death of an old person, the abandonment of a village by a farmer, determines the disappearance of a library of local knowledge indispensable to face new challenges.»

Africa Forum, Rome 2008

Baglio San Michele stands within a centuries-old tradition of Mediterranean rural settlements. The fortified farmhouse, or baglio, was never merely an agricultural estate. It constituted a small productive and relational cosmos: a quadrangular complex organised around a central courtyard that served as the heart of family, working and community life.

In the courtyard produce was gathered and worked, labourers and family members gathered, daily activities took place. The thick walls of local stone guaranteed thermal inertia; orientation and enclosure protected from sun and wind; cisterns collected rainwater. Every element responded to a logic of balance between man, territory and resources.

This constructive and social wisdom, heir to Greek, Roman, Arab and Norman influences, produced over the centuries self-sustaining vital niches: settlements capable of perpetuating and regenerating themselves in respect of natural cycles and the characteristics of the place. The Baglio San Michele project does not invent a climate-control system, but rediscovers and adapts ancient principles: thermal comfort, autonomy, beauty and continuity with the culture of the Sicilian Mediterranean.

The genealogy of the baglio

واحة

Wāḥa

Mediterranean deserts · From the 4th millennium BC

The oasis is not born of nature. It is born of the decision of a human group to stay — and to work together to wrest fertility from the sand. Rainwater collection, underground channels, trees planted in sequence to create microclimates: every oasis is a collective project of survival. The oasis teaches that a place does not exist without the community that wants it.

Αὐλή

Aulé

Classical Greece · 5th–4th century BC

In ancient Olynthus, houses opened inward, not toward the street. The central courtyard — the aulé — oriented to the south received the low winter sun and was shaded in summer by the overhang of the roof. It was not architecture for the rich. It was intelligence applied to climate, within everyone's reach.

Atrium

Rome · 2nd century BC – 5th century AD

The Roman domus adds two elements to the Greek principle: the atrium, which collects rainwater in a central basin — the impluvium — and the peristyle. Water collected flowed into cisterns under the floor and was used for irrigation. No waste, no external system: the building was autonomous because it had been designed to be so.

حوش

Ḥawsh

Arab-Norman Sicily · 9th–12th century

The Arabs bring to Sicily the desert version of the same principle: the house closes toward the outside as a compact block, thick walls that insulate from the heat, and opens toward the sky through an internal courtyard — the حوش (ḥawsh) — where the water of a fountain cools the air by evaporation and vegetation filters the light. The cultural axis Andalusia–Arab-Norman Sicily finds here one of its most direct expressions on the territory.

Baglio

Western Sicily · 15th–18th century

The baglio is the final synthesis. Walls of local limestone, high and thick, which in summer keep the cool and in winter radiate the accumulated heat. A single portal — defensive toward the outside, welcoming toward the inside. A closed courtyard that is at once space for work, meeting and rest. Underground cisterns. Loggias and porticoes. And always a space for the sacred: the chapel. The baglio was not a palace. It was a system: self-sufficient, communal, designed to last generations.

The Moorish-Arab contribution

There is no single monument called “Moorish Baglio”, but there exists a typological variant of the Sicilian baglio — especially in western and central-western Sicily — strongly marked by the Arab-Islamic building tradition. This is the matrix we precisely call the Moorish-Arab contribution.

The distinctive elements are the compact form closed toward the outside, the internal courtyard (حوش ḥawsh) as the only large open space, the sophisticated management of water at ground level, the massive masonry of high thermal inertia, the reduced and strategic openings, and the absence of prestige towers or purely decorative apparatus. This matrix is not a generic “oriental inspiration”: it is a technical-climatic system elaborated in an arid Mediterranean environment and then transmitted and hybridised in Sicily.

The most pertinent cultural axis is not Persia or India, but the concrete axis Islamic Andalusia → Maghreb → Arab-Norman Sicily. In the great Andalusian examples (Alhambra, Generalife, Alcázar of Seville) and in the Zisa Palace in Palermo, the patio with فسقية (fisqiya) and سلسبيل (salsabīl) is a machine of evaporative cooling. In the countryside the same principle is translated into a more sober and productive form: the حوش of the Arab house and, later, the courtyard of the baglio.

The baglio of Moorish-Arab matrix historically demonstrates that thermal comfort is obtained without systems, through geometry, mass, water and air; that the courtyard is a social and climatic device at the same time; and that self-sufficiency and sober beauty arise from the same logic. This is exactly the point that Baglio San Michele intends to reactivate.

The long history of the organised rural residence shows, moreover, how structures born with a defensive character often evolved into hybrid organisms, capable of uniting protection, agricultural production and ordered family life, thus becoming not only economic units but also the expression of an idea of dwelling rooted in the territory and destined to perpetuate itself through the centuries.

The courtyard as climatic and social device

At the centre of the traditional baglio there is not merely an empty space, but a complex device, at once climatic and social. The internal courtyard, heir to the Mediterranean and Arab-Norman patio house, performed an essential thermoregulatory function: it protected from direct sun and prevailing winds, favoured natural ventilation and created a more temperate microclimate than the outside.

The cultural axis of reference is clear and pertinent: from Islamic Spain to the Maghreb as far as Arab-Norman Sicily. In the great Andalusian examples (Alhambra and Generalife) and in the Zisa Palace in Palermo, the patio is not only formal space, but a climatic machine. Water at low level — the فسقية (fisqiya, courtyard basin) and floor channels — favours evaporation and cools the air. The perimeter walls, thick and of local stone, accumulate daytime heat and slowly release it during the night hours, thanks to their thermal inertia.

But the courtyard was also the place where common life unfolded. Productive activities, moments of shared work and family relations converged there. It constituted the intermediate space between the private dimension of the house and the collective dimension of agricultural labour. This dual nature — climatic and relational — offers today a precious model. At Baglio San Michele the courtyard will not be a decorative element, but the ordering principle of community life.

Water management and hydraulic wisdom

In a territory marked by scarcity of water resources, the survival of Sicilian rural settlements has historically been linked to refined and pragmatic hydraulic wisdom. Cisterns dug or built to collect rainwater from the roofs, drainage and distribution channels, wells and reuse techniques constituted an integrated system for managing the most precious resource.

In the Arab-Andalusian and Arab-Norman tradition this principle reaches particularly refined forms. The سلسبيل (salsabīl) — a fountain with an inclined surface that maximises the area of water exposed to the air — and the ground-level basins (فسقية fisqiya) are not ornaments: they are evaporative cooling devices. The Zisa Palace in Palermo offers an extraordinary and direct example on Sicilian soil: floor-level fountains, channels and air paths that cool the rooms without any mechanical system.

This attention was not only technical: scarcity generated collective responsibility. Care of water became a social fact, foundation of shared rules and solidarity.

In the Baglio San Michele project this lesson is taken as a principle. The restoration of the Masseria and the subsequent phases of development provide for the recovery and adaptation of traditional collection and conservation systems, together with a central ground-level fountain with channels and a سلسبيل (salsabīl) in Hyblaean stone in the entrance hall, so that water autonomy and thermal comfort become concrete practice of a community that intends to inhabit the territory with sobriety and foresight.

Movement of air and vertical devices

Alongside evaporation, the second fundamental principle of Mediterranean natural climate control is the controlled movement of air.

The بادگیر (bādgīr, wind tower) of the Persian and Maghrebi tradition captures the wind at height and channels it downward, exploiting differences in pressure and temperature. It is not a matter of copying these towers literally, which are foreign to the Hyblaean landscape. It is a matter of understanding the principle and reinterpreting it in a discreet and local form.

At Baglio San Michele this principle is translated into:

  • the existing vertical path (entrance hall – stairs – terrace) as a natural chimney for extracting hot air;
  • discreet chimneys and ventilation openings, designed according to the logic of the بادگیر (bādgīr) but integrated into the sober language of the masseria;
  • calibrated openings that favour cross-ventilation without compromising the thermal inertia of the walls.

Evaporation (سلسبيل / فسقية) and air movement (vertical paths + بادگیر-type elements) work together, as already happened in the best examples of the Mediterranean tradition. In winter the system is completed by stoves, fireplaces and the same thermal mass of the walls.

The baglio as a self-sufficient ecosystem

The fortified farmhouse was not an isolated productive island, but a small ecosystem in which every element was in relation with the others. Agricultural production, livestock, craftsmanship, dwelling and family life took place within a unitary organism, regulated by the cycles of nature and by the need to optimise available resources.

Productive cycle

Animals provided labour and manure; gardens and fields fed people and beasts; wall structures and open spaces responded to needs of defence, work and daily life.

Work and craftsmanship

Manual work and craftsmanship were not accessory activities, but an integral part of the quality of the place and of its capacity to endure over time.

Territorial intelligence

This model of relative self-sufficiency represented a form of territorial intelligence, today more necessary than ever in the face of the fragility of external supply chains.

Today, in an era of dependence on external systems and fragility of supply chains, recovering this vision means designing a community capable of producing, at least in part, its own food, of valuing manual work and of maintaining a direct and responsible relationship with the land that hosts it. Baglio San Michele proposes itself as a contemporary interpretation of that ecosystem: not closure, but autonomy; not isolation, but rootedness.

Continuity with local knowledge

Recovering the knowledge of the Mediterranean tradition does not mean claiming to repeat the past or to compose an eclectic mosaic of stylistic citations. It means, rather, recognising in them a heritage of consolidated, culturally rooted and ecologically effective knowledge, and reinterpreting them in the light of present needs.

As Lorena Musotto wrote in her research on sustainable settlements of the Mediterranean tradition, it is a matter of organising self-sustaining and autopoietic social models, capable of perpetuating and regenerating themselves in respect of the climatic, geographic and historical-cultural characteristics of the place.

The restoration of Baglio San Michele is therefore not an operation of scenic prestige, but an act of identity affirmation and responsibility. The courtyard, the massive masonry, the management of water, the organisation of work and of common life are taken up not as ornaments of the past, but as operational principles still valid.

In this way the intentional community that will take shape here does not interrupt the history of the territory, but continues it with awareness and responsibility. The Baglio is not a museum. It is a living organism that, starting from its historical roots, seeks to answer the questions of our time with the wisdom of those who already knew how to inhabit this territory in a lasting and beautiful way.

Decline and rebirth

After the Unification of Italy the rural residential structure entered into crisis. Baglio San Michele interprets its historical meaning and proposes a radical rebirth, coherent with tradition and oriented toward stable community life.

Crisis and transformation

After Unification, the traditional villa and the baglio entered into crisis: large property appeared too slow in yielding returns and bound to archaic mentalities. The historical response was often the scenic recovery of residences. Baglio San Michele operates an analogous but inverted dynamic: it does not modernise an aristocratic architecture to turn it into a prestigious summer residence, but restores a fortified rural organism (the Masseria Torre Mastro) in order to give it a new stable community life, oriented toward family, faith and autonomy.

Historical reflection and identity

Historical reflection is a tool for affirming collective values: lineage, tradition, continuity. Architecture becomes a language of rootedness. This is the ideational core of the project: the Baglio is not a generic housing intervention, but an act of rootedness in Sicilian culture and Christian tradition. Maintaining and enhancing the form of the baglio (courtyard, walls, relationship with the countryside) means looking back in order to affirm a living bond with the past, not mere nostalgia.

Recovery and new design

Phase 1 is almost entirely a major intervention of recovery and internal reorganisation of the existing baglio (14 apartments). Phase 2 provides for new rural constructions on the masseria’s lands and the restoration of the collapsed wing. The historical lesson is clear: when building anew, it is essential to maintain coherence with the Sicilian typology and with the idea of a traditional intentional community.

Craftsmanship and the borgo

The happy union of design and craftsmanship is a typical expression of the borgo. Baglio San Michele is not only a container of dwellings, but an organism that brings back to life constructive knowledge, manual work and handmade beauty, in continuity with local tradition. This is exactly what the Archivum Artium and cooperation with the craft and entrepreneurial realities of the Hyblaean territory aim at.

Baglio San Michele

Baglio San Michele is one of these places. Built in the 16th century in the Ragusa territory, it carries in its stone the memory of all this.

Its walls of local calcarenite have an average thickness of eighty centimetres: not for ostentation, but because that thermal mass keeps the internal temperature relatively constant all year round, without systems. The central courtyard was the heart of common life — work, meals, festivals, assemblies. The underground cistern collected winter rainwater and returned it in the dry months. The chapel marked the rhythm of time.

In the restoration these principles are reactivated in concrete and sober form:

  • central ground-level fountain and channels → principle of the فسقية (fisqiya);
  • سلسبيل (salsabīl) in Hyblaean stone in the entrance hall → maximisation of evaporation;
  • vertical path (entrance hall–stairs–terrace) as natural chimney;
  • discreet chimneys and ventilation openings inspired by the بادگیر (bādgīr) principle, without foreign stylistic citations;
  • local materials, sober language, absence of exotic ornaments.

In winter the system is completed by stoves, fireplaces and the same thermal mass of the walls.

It was not nostalgia. It was competence: the knowledge accumulated over five thousand years of Mediterranean experience on how to build a space in which one lives well with little, where relationships between people and the relationship with the land are not added values but structural conditions of functioning.

The Borgo Ideale project is not restoring a monument. It is reactivating a system.

The thesis of the project

Baglio San Michele is not a historic building to be preserved.
It is a millennial technology to be put back into operation.
